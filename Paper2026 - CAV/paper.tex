\begin{abstract}
Solving parity games is a cornerstone problem in formal verification and synthesis, with significant applications across industrial-scale system design. We present NOCQ, an efficient constraint-based toolchain designed to solve parity games under quantitative conditions. By leveraging the inherent duality of parity game players, NOCQ implements several constraint programming (CP) techniques that exploit search space symmetries. Our experimental evaluation demonstrates that NOCQ performs competitively against state-of-the-art specialized solvers—often matching or exceeding their performance—while maintaining the modularity and flexibility of a CP-based framework. This flexibility allows users to easily incorporate additional constraints to search for preferred solutions, such as Mean-payoff requirements. We describe the tool's architecture, provide a comprehensive performance analysis, and discuss its extensibility. NOCQ is open-source and available at: \url{https://github.com/gonzalohernandez/nocq}.

\keywords{Constraint programming \and Parity games \and Verification.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Parity games are two-player zero-sum infinite-duration games played on a labelled directed graph, where vertices represent states of the players in the game and edges represent their possible available actions at every state. 
Solving these games is central to several techniques in automated formal verification, in particular, when solving model checking and automated synthesis problems.

Since parity games are zero-sum, perfect-information games, solving them amounts to finding which player (usually called Player Even, or $\even$, and Player Odd, or $\odd$) has a winning strategy, from a given starting vertex in the graph. 
It is known that deciding the winner in parity games is an $\mathsf{NP} \cap \mathsf{co\textit{-}NP}$ problem~\cite{Jurdziński:1998}, but it is not known whether or not they can be solved in polynomial time; a problem that has remained open for nearly 50 years. 
% (Done) Remove Arcucci's reference and add, instead, a reference to priority promotion and the latest result on its coplexity, where it is shown that they are quasi-polynomial, especially this last one is important as it is the main complexity result at the moment. 
Due to the importance of parity games in formal verification, several algorithms have been developed \cite{Aggarwal:2023,Benerecetti:2025,Jurdziński:2000,Jurdziński:2022,Zielonka:1998}, all of them suffering from exponential running time limitations for specific classes of games. 
One such approach involves reducing the solution of a parity game to the solution of a SAT instance representing the game. The generated SAT instance is satisfiable if and only if Player Even has a winning strategy in the original game. 
This idea has a lot of potential given the advances in SAT-solving technology, but, as we will show later, it suffers from poor scalability due to the large size of the generated CNF formula for a given game.

In this paper, we address these scalability issues by developing a specialised propagator that takes into account the structure of parity games, resulting in significant improvements in performance and modelling power. 
In particular, we show that our method outperforms all other state-of-the-art constraint-based solvers.
%
Specifically, we propose replacing the SAT-based encoding used to forbid cycles corresponding to Player Odd wins in the game graph \cite{Heljanko:2012} with a set of efficient global constraint propagators; this significantly reduces the encoding size of the problem. Two of the proposed propagators are based on depth-first search, and a third is a checker that analyses the strongly connected components (SCCs) of the game graph. These propagators filter edges that would close winning cycles for a selected opponent player and generate explanations for each filtering step, enabling their integration into a Lazy Clause Generation solver~\cite{DBLP:journals/constraints/OhrimenkoSC09}.

We show that our implementation not only outperforms existing SAT and CP-based approaches but also achieves performance comparable to that of state-of-the-art specialized non-CP-based algorithms, often matching or exceeding their results. This work contributes to establishing the CP framework as a practical tool for solving both general and specialized classes of parity games.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We first present the theoretical foundations and necessary definitions used in further sections, and then introduce our proposed approach -- including the design and implementation of the \noc propagators. After that, we provide different experimental results, a comparative analysis against state-of-the-art solvers, and present the extension of our method with additional constraints. At the end of the paper, we focus on the presentation of a number of concluding remarks, a discussion of our main findings, and some proposals for potential future research directions.

\section{Background and Overview}

This section introduces parity games with quantitative conditions and overviews the existing translations into Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) and Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT).

\subsection{Arenas, Strategies, and Plays}

A game arena is a tuple $\mathcal{A} = (V_{\subeven}, V_{\subodd}, E, \Omega, \Lambda)$, where $V = V_{\subeven} \cup V_{\subodd}$ is a finite set of vertices partitioned between Player \even ($0$) and Player \odd ($1$), $E \subseteq V \times V$ is a set of directed edges where every vertex has at least one successor, $\Omega: V \to \{0, \dots, d\}$ is a priority mapping, and $\Lambda: E \to \mathbb{Z}$ is a weight function.

A \textit{positional strategy} for player $i \in \{\even, \odd\}$ is a function $f_{i} : V_{i} \to V$ such that $(v, f_i(v)) \in E$. A pair of strategies $(f_{\subeven}, f_{\subodd})$ uniquely determines an infinite path $\pi = v_0 v_1 \dots$, called a \textit{play}. We denote by $\text{Inf}(\pi)$ the set of priorities $\{\Omega(v) \mid v \text{ occurs infinitely often in } \pi\}$.

\subsection{Winning Conditions}

In our setting, a play $\pi$ is evaluated against three distinct criteria:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Parity Condition ($\gamma$):} Satisfied if $\max(\text{Inf}(\pi))$ is even.
    \item \textbf{Energy Condition ($\eta$):} Given initial energy $E_{init} \in \mathbb{N}$, satisfied if $\forall n \ge 0: E_{init} + \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \Lambda(v_i, v_{i+1}) \ge 0$.
    \item \textbf{Mean-Payoff Condition ($\mu$):} Satisfied if $\liminf_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \Lambda(v_i, v_{i+1}) \ge 0$.
\end{enumerate}

We define the winning objective for Player \even as the conjunction of these conditions: $\Xi_{\subeven} = \gamma \wedge \eta \wedge \mu$. As these are zero-sum games, Player \odd wins if the play satisfies the objective $\Xi_{\subodd} = \neg \Xi_{\subeven}$.

Given an initial vertex $v_0$, a strategy is said to be winning from $v_0$ for player~$\even$ (player~$\odd$) if it only generates winning plays for player~$\even$ (player~$\odd$) starting at $v_0$ for all strategies of the other player. It is known that for every vertex~$v\in V$ in a parity game, one of the two players always has a memoryless winning strategy from that vertex~$v$. Solving a parity game means deciding, for every vertex~$v\in V$, which player has a memoryless winning strategy from that vertex.

\subsection{Solving Parity Games via SMT/SAT Encodings}

Beyond dedicated algorithms, parity games can be solved by encoding them into constraint-based formalisms like SAT or SMT~\cite{Heljanko:2012}. This approach reduces the existence of a winning strategy for player \even to the satisfiability of a formula $\Phi$. We define Boolean variables $S_v$ for $v \in V$ and $T_{v,w}$ for $(v, w) \in E$, representing whether vertices and edges belong to a winning strategy for Player~0. The structural winning conditions are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $\Phi_\exists = \bigwedge_{v \in V_{\subeven}} (S_v \rightarrow \bigvee_{w \in vE} T_{(v,w)})$
    \item $\Phi_\forall = \bigwedge_{v \in V_{\subodd}} (S_v \rightarrow \bigwedge_{w \in vE} T_{(v,w)})$
    \item $\Phi_V = \bigwedge_{w \in V, w \neq v_0} \left( \left(\bigvee_{v \in Ev} T_{(v,w)}\right) \rightarrow S_w \right)$
\end{itemize}


$\Phi_\exists$ and $\Phi_\forall$ ensure valid moves for player \even and \odd, respectively, while $\Phi_V$ ensures consistency between edge and vertex selection. To satisfy the parity condition $\gamma$, we employ a \textit{discrete progress measure}~\cite{Jurdziński:2000} to rule out cycles where the maximum priority is odd. We introduce integer variables $x_p^v$ for each $v \in V$ and each odd priority $p \in \text{Odd}(G)$, where $\text{Odd}^{>k}(G) = \{p \in \text{Odd}(G) \mid p > k\}$. The progress constraints are $\Phi_A = \bigwedge_{(v,w) \in E} (T_{v,w} \to \Psi_{v,w})$, where:

\[
\Psi_{v,w} = 
\begin{cases} 
\bigwedge_{p \in \text{Odd}^{>\Omega(w)}} (x_p^v \geq x_p^w) & \text{if } \Omega(w) \text{ is even} \\
(x_{\Omega(w)}^v > x_{\Omega(w)}^w) \land \bigwedge_{p \in \text{Odd}^{>\Omega(w)}} (x_p^v \geq x_p^w) & \text{if } \Omega(w) \text{ is odd}
\end{cases}
\]

These constraints belong to \textit{difference logic}, supported by SMT solvers. The satisfiability of $S_{v_0} \wedge \Phi_\exists \wedge \Phi_\forall \wedge \Phi_V \wedge \Phi_A$ determines if player \even wins from $v_0$. Alternatively, $\Phi_A$ can be bit-blasted into pure CNF for use with modern SAT solvers~\cite{Heljanko:2012}.

\subsection{Solving Quantitative Parity Games via Efficient \noc Propagators}

We solve quantitative parity games by encoding them into a CSP with a specialized propagator.

\begin{definition}\label{def:csp}
    The CSP for an arena $G=(V,E, \Omega, \Lambda)$ starting at $v_0$ from the perspective of player $\player$ is $\mathcal{P} = (\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{D}, \mathcal{C})$, where $\mathcal{X} = \{ \mathcal{V}_v \}_{v \in V} \cup \{ \mathcal{E}_e \}_{e \in E}$ are Boolean variables. The constraints $\mathcal{C}$ are:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\mathcal{C}_{v_0} : \mathcal{V}_{v_0} = \top$
        \item $\mathcal{C}_{\player} : \bigwedge_{v \in V_{\player}} (\mathcal{V}_v \rightarrow (\sum_{w \in vE} \mathcal{E}_{(v,w)}= 1))$
        \item $\mathcal{C}_{\opponent} : \bigwedge_{v \in V_{\opponent}} (\mathcal{V}_v \rightarrow \bigwedge_{w \in vE} \mathcal{E}_{(v,w)})$
        \item $\mathcal{C}_{\!E} : \bigwedge_{w \in V, w \neq v_0} ((\bigvee_{v \in Ew} \mathcal{E}_{(v,w)}) \rightarrow \mathcal{V}_w)$
        \item $\mathcal{C}_{{\!N\!O\!C}_\gamma}  : \bigwedge_{v_0, \dots, v_k} ((\bigwedge_{i\in 0\dots k-1} \mathcal{E}_{(v_i,v_{i+1})}) \rightarrow \bigwedge_{j\in 0\dots k-1}\\ (v_j=v_k \rightarrow 
            \max\{\Omega(v_i) \mid i\in j\dots k\} \bmod 2 \ne \opponent))$
        
        \item $\mathcal{C}_{{\!N\!O\!C}_\eta}  : \bigwedge_{v_0, \dots, v_k} ((\bigwedge_{i\in 0\dots k-1} \mathcal{E}_{(v_i,v_{i+1})}) \rightarrow \bigwedge_{j\in 0\dots k-1}\\ (v_j=v_k \rightarrow 
            \sum_{i=j}^{k} \Lambda(v_i, v_{i+1}) \ge 0))$
        
        \item $\mathcal{C}_{{\!N\!O\!C}_\mu}  : \bigwedge_{v_0, \dots, v_k} ((\bigwedge_{i\in 0\dots k-1} \mathcal{E}_{(v_i,v_{i+1})}) \rightarrow \bigwedge_{j\in 0\dots k-1}\\ (v_j=v_k \rightarrow 
            \frac{1}{k-j+1} \sum_{i=j}^{k} \Lambda(v_i, v_{i+1}) \ge \nu ))$
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

While we retain the connectivity constraints $\mathcal{C}_{v_0}, \mathcal{C}_{\opponent}$, and $\mathcal{C}_{\!E}$ (analogous to $\Phi_{v_0}, \Phi_\forall$, and $\Phi_V$), and $\mathcal{C}_{\player}$ enforces that $\player$ selects exactly one strategy edge per vertex, we observe that progress measure constraints $\Phi_A$ are computationally expensive, scaling up to $O(|V|^3)$—and can generate hundreds of millions of clauses for games with only 5,000 nodes. Our approach replaces $\Phi_A$ with a global constraint that avoids the use of new variables and rules out cycles winning for $\opponent$.

The global constraint $\mathcal{C}_{\!N\!O\!C}$ ensures that every reachable cycle is winning for $\player$ by satisfying a combination of parity ($\rho$), energy ($\eta$), and mean-payoff ($\mu$) requirements. This generalized construction allows \nocq to search from the perspective of either player by swapping $\player$ and $\opponent$. Satisfiability under $\player$ proves a winning strategy for $\player$, while unsatisfiability (if the solver explores the full space) proves the win for $\opponent$.


\subsubsection{A No-Opponent-Cycle filter} \label{sec:nocfilter}
We have proposed a complete propagator based on Depth-first Search (DFS) to explore the arena. The resulting DFS tree captures all possible paths from $v_0$, and can be used to detect all existing cycles. We define the propagator in the context of a \textit{Lazy Clause Generation} solver~\cite{DBLP:journals/constraints/OhrimenkoSC09}, to take advantage of the strong performance of this type of solver, in particular for problems that contain a large number of Boolean clauses. Algorithm~\ref{alg:noc-filter} presents this version of the propagator, which checks for cycles and reports failure if a cycle with highest priority in favour of $\opponent$ is detected.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\small
\caption{\textbf{\textit{NOCFilter}}}
\textbf{Input}: $path_V,\ path_E,\ v,\ e,\ \mathcal{E},\ \textit{defined}$ \\
\textbf{Output}: $\top$ is valid; $\bot$ otherwise
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \IF{$v \in path_V$} \label{alg:noc-filter:pathcheck}
        \STATE $i_v \gets \textit{index}(v, path_V)$
        \STATE $m \gets \max\{f(v') \mid v' \in path_V[i_v:]\}$
        \IF{$\neg \Xi_{\player}$}
        % \IF{$\neg \Xi_{\even} \lor \neg \Xi_{\odd}$}
        % \IF{($\player = \even \land \neg(\gamma \land \eta \land \mu)) \lor (\player = \odd \land (\gamma \land \eta \land \mu)$)}
        % \IF{$\neg satisfyConditions(i_v, path_V, path_E)$} \label{alg:noc-eager:checkopponent}
            \STATE $\textit{reason} \gets \textit{clausify}(\neg path_E[i_v:])$ \label{alg:noc-eager:reason}
            \STATE \textbf{if }not ($\mathcal{E}[e] \gets (\bot, \textit{reason}))$ \textbf{then return $\bot$}
        \ENDIF
    \ELSIF{$\textit{defined}$} \label{alg:noc-eager:defcheck}
        \FOR{$e' \in \textit{edges}(v) \textbf{ where } \mathcal{E}[e'] \ne \bot$} \label{alg:no-opponent-cycle-f1:edgecheck}
            \STATE $p_V \gets path_V \cup \{v\}$
            \STATE $p_E \gets path_E \cup \{e'\}$
            \STATE $w \gets \textit{target}(e')$
            \STATE $\textit{def} \gets \mathcal{E}[e'] = \top$
            \STATE \textit{NOCEager}(
                $p_V$,
                $p_E$,
                $w$,
                $e'$,
                $\mathcal{E}$,
                $\textit{def}$\ )
        \ENDFOR
    \ENDIF
    \RETURN $\top$
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:noc-filter}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:noc-filter} is written in a recursive style. The arguments $\mathit{path}_V$ and $\mathit{path}_E$ represent the currently explored path in the DFS procedure, expressed in terms of both vertices and edges. The parameter $v$ denotes the current vertex, $e$ is the currently explored edge, and $\mathcal{E}$ is the array of Boolean variables associated with the edges of the graph. The \noc propagator invokes this function as $\textit{NOCFilter}(\emptyset,\ \emptyset,\ start,\ -1,\ \mathcal{E},\ \top)$, where –1 indicates that no edge has been selected yet from $\mathcal{E}$, as valid edges are indexed with $e \geq 0$.

The complexity \nocfilter is primarily driven by the DFS traversal over the graph, with additional work performed when a cycle is detected. In the worst case, the algorithm explores every simple path in the graph starting from the initial vertex. This may result in an exponential number of recursive calls in the number of vertices since each branching decision adds a new path to explore. However, in practice, the number of recursive calls is bounded by the number of edges assigned to $\top$ and at most one unassigned edge, significantly reducing the branching factor. Each recursive call performs a constant number of operations, except when a cycle is found (line~\ref{alg:noc-filter:pathcheck}). Nevertheless, because the algorithm may visit multiple paths and cycles, and because each edge can potentially trigger multiple clause generation steps before being fixed, the overall worst-case complexity remains exponential in the size of the graph. This trade-off is justified by the early pruning capabilities enabled by detecting potentially winning opponent cycles before they are fully formed, especially in graphs with many unassigned edges or nondeterminism. In typical solver applications, where assignments propagate quickly and many edges are fixed early, the practical runtime of the propagator is significantly better than the theoretical worst case—as demonstrated by the experimental results presented in section \ref{sec:experiments}, where this approach consistently outperforms alternatives in both speed and effectiveness.

\subsection{Solving for $\player$ and $\opponent$ in Parallel}

As our experiments will demonstrate, solver performance is heavily dependent on the game's underlying structure. A key observation is the asymmetry in computational cost inherent in game structure: properties that challenge $\player$'s solution often simplify $\opponent$'s, and vice versa. This problem duality allows us to transform and simplify the solution. By exploiting this, we can avoid a worst-case scenario for one player by solving the game from the opposite perspective. Consequently, solving the game from both perspectives in parallel significantly improves overall efficiency. This acceleration is largely attributed to the \noc propagator's capacity to rapidly prune the search space by eliminating $\opponent$'s winning options, effectively proving $\player$ has no winning strategy. We execute both perspectives concurrently and utilize the result obtained by the faster computation.

As confirmed by additional experiments (available on the \href{https://figshare.com/s/38b43592b1047a9b893a}{Online Appendix}), we confirm that state-of-the-art methods cannot exploit this duality, demonstrating consistent performance regardless of which player wins.

\subsection{Games with quantitative conditions}

As we will see in this section, our method flexibly integrates additional constraints—such as quantitative or structural requirements—without any modification to the core algorithm. This inherent adaptability to evaluate additional conditions is a decisive advantage of this method while others necessitate custom adaptations for every additional set of constraints in the problem.

\section{Tool Description}

In this section, we describe the implementation of NOCQ, a C++ framework developed to model and solve Parity Games with quantitative conditions. Unlike Oink \cite{vanDijk:2018}, which focuses on explicit-state and symbolic algorithms, NOCQ is designed to leverage constraint programming (CP) technologies.

\subsection*{The Core Architecture and Solvers}
NOCQ implements the CSP model described in Definition~\ref{def:csp} and integrates the \noc propagators outlined in Section~\ref{sec:nocfilter}. A key design principle of NOCQ is solver independence; the underlying model can be interfaced with various CP engines. As a proof of concept, we have integrated two state-of-the-art solvers:

Chuffed \cite{chuffed:2018}: A lazy clause generation solver.

Gecode \cite{Gecode:2019}: A comprehensive constraint-based toolkit.

The architecture is modular, allowing new solvers to be integrated via the cp-nocq directory. Additionally, NOCQ provides a SAT encoding path, allowing parity games to be dispatched to high-performance SAT solvers such as CaDiCaL \cite{Biere:Cadical:2024}, Kissat \cite{Biere:Kissat:2024}, or MiniSat \cite{Een:MiniSat:2003}.

\subsection*{Game Generation and Input Formats}
NOCQ includes a built-in generator for standard benchmarks, including Random games, Jurdziński games, and MLadder games, following the algorithms proposed by \cite{Keiren:2015}. For external benchmarks, the tool supports two primary input formats:

GM: The standard format used in the PGSolver and Oink suites \cite{Friedmann:2017,Keiren:2015}.

DZN: The data format used in MiniZinc \cite{Minizinc:2020} for constraint modeling.

Both formats have been extended within NOCQ to support edge weights, enabling the modeling of parity games with quantitative constraints (e.g., energy and mean-payoff conditions).

\subsection*{Extending to new quantitative conditions}

NOCQ is designed to be easily extensible for new quantitative conditions. To add a new condition, developers can overload the class WinningCondition in the similar way as the existing EnergyCondition and MeanPayoffCondition classes, and included it in the CSP model. The modular design allows for seamless integration of new constraints without altering the core solving algorithms.

\section{Experiments and Evaluation}\label{sec:experiments}

To evaluate our approach, we implemented the \noc propagators using the Constraint Programming (CP) solver Chuffed (version 0.12.1) \cite{chuffed:2018}. We utilized MiniZinc \cite{DBLP:conf/cp/NethercoteSBBDT07} to model additional constraints in selected experiments.

For comparison, we employed several external state-of-the-art solvers: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item SAT Solvers (Reduction SAT): We used \textbf{CaDiCaL} (v2.1.3) \cite{Biere:Cadical:2024}, \textbf{MiniSat} (v2.2.0) \cite{Een:MiniSat:2003}, \textbf{Kissat} (v4.0.2) \cite{Biere:Kissat:2024}, and zChaff (v2007.3.12) \cite{Moskewicz:zChaff:2001}.
    \item State-of-the-Art (SOTA) Parity Game Solvers: We incorporated Oink (fresh GitHub version) \cite{vanDijk:2018}, which includes several high-performance algorithms like Priority Promotion ($\textbf{PP}$ and $\textbf{PP+}$) and Parys' improvements ($\textbf{PAR}$). We also included the Zielonka Recursive Algorithm ($\textbf{ZRA}$), with an improved attractor strategy as detailed in \cite{Aggarwal:2023}, which offers better perfonmance. 
\end{itemize}

All experiments were conducted on an Intel Xeon 8260 CPU (24 cores, non-hyperthreaded) with 268.55 GB of RAM, running Linux. The complete source code, including implementations and experimental setups, is available at 
\cite{Hernandez:Github:NoOppnentCycle:2025}.

We evaluated all algorithms using a standard benchmark set commonly employed in parity game research, drawing from the PGSolver Library \cite{Friedmann:2017} and the Benchmarks for Parity Games \cite{Keiren:2015}. The games were classified into four categories:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Equivalence Checking ($\textbf{EqC}$): This group contains 77 games derived from formal equivalence checking problems, including strong bisimulation, weak bisimulation, and stuttering equivalence. These games are characterized by complex, structured connectivity patterns reflecting system design.
    \item Model Checking ($\textbf{MoC}$): This set consists of 106 games originating from temporal logic model checking ($\mu$-calculus) against system models. These instances are considered organic and reflect real-world verification challenges.
    \item PGSolver Collection ($\textbf{PGS}$): This collection comprises 161 games sourced from the hardest known families of parity games, including ladder games, recursive ladder games, elevator verification, clique games, steady games, and Jurdziński games \cite{Benerecetti:2016}, likely to expose some of the worst performance.
    \item Random Games ($\textbf{RaG}$): This category includes 125 randomly generated games with varying sizes and priority distributions, designed primarily to test the general robustness and average-case performance of solving algorithms.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Automated Synthesis of Reactive Systems}

In (local) model checking, it suffices to determine the winner from a given vertex (satisfiable if won by Player~$\even$, unsatisfiable if won by Player~$\odd$). However, applications like automated synthesis~\cite{Bruyere:2022,Finkbeiner:2018,Luttenberger:2020} require explicit winning strategies, that is, a description of the decisions to be made by the winner at each vertex.

Our satisfiability encoding can capture multiple winning plays simultaneously. For instance, in Figure~\ref{fig:game02b}, a solution assigning $\top$ to vertices $\mathcal{V}_0$ to $\mathcal{V}_3$ and edges $\mathcal{E}_0$, $\mathcal{E}_1$, $\mathcal{E}_2$, $\mathcal{E}_4$, and $\mathcal{E}_6$ captures two distinct plays.
%
To extract individual winning plays, we extend the CSP with variables and constraints such that each solution corresponds to exactly one play. We define a sequence of integer variables $(\mathcal{P}_s)_{s \in \{0..\mid V \mid - 1\}}$ to represent the path. More specifically, we also add:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathsf{h}$: the position where the finite prefix ends;

\item $\mathsf{t}$: the start of the cycle (tail).
\end{itemize} 

\noindent 
We then introduce three new constraints:
\begin{itemize}
\item ($\mathcal{C}_{\textit{path}}$): 
$\mathsf{t} \in \{0..\mid V \mid -1\} \wedge \bigwedge_{s \in \{1..\mathsf{t}\}} \bigvee_{e=(v,w) \in E} (\mathcal{E}_e \wedge \mathcal{P}_{s-1} = v \wedge \mathcal{P}_s = w)$;
This ensures each step follows a valid edge;
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\item ($\mathcal{C}_{\textit{cycle}}$): 
$\mathsf{h} \in \{0..\mathsf{t}\} \wedge \mathcal{P}_{\mathsf{t}+1} = \mathcal{P}_\mathsf{h}$; \\
It ensures the cycle closes at position $\mathsf{t} + 1$;
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\item ($\mathcal{C}_{\textit{unique}}$): 
$\bigwedge_{i \neq j \in \{0..\mathsf{t}\}} (\mathcal{P}_i \neq \mathcal{P}_j)$; \\
This constraint enforces only one cycle.
\end{itemize} 

These constraints can be added in MiniZinc without modifying the core algorithm of \noc. Using Chuffed as the backend, we enumerate all solutions to obtain the set of winning plays, represented as vertex sequences. 

\subsection{Energy Conditions/Games}

We now consider games with additional quantitative objectives. In \textit{Energy Games}~\cite{Chatterjee:2010}, each edge carries an energy level (a weight), and a play is winning if the running sum of these levels remains non-negative at all times, in addition to satisfying the parity condition.
%
%For memoryless parity games, 
This requirement can be encoded using the $\mathcal{P}$ variables introduced earlier. Given a set of energy levels $(L_e)_{e \in E}$, we define integer variables $(\mathcal{I}_s)_{s \in \{0..\mathsf{t} - \mathsf{h} + 1\}}$ to represent the edges in the infinite cycle (tail) of the play:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal{C}_{\textit{infEdges}} : \forall s \in \{0..\mathsf{t} - \mathsf{h} + 1\} (\mathcal{I}_s = E(\mathcal{P}_{s+\mathsf{h}}, \mathcal{P}_{s+\mathsf{h}+1}))$
\end{itemize}

We then impose the following constraint to ensure the total energy level in the cycle is non-negative:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal{C}_{\textit{energy}} : \sum_{e \in \mathcal{I}} L_e \geq 0$
\end{itemize}

While a complete model would also enforce energy constraints over all finite prefixes (up to $\mathsf{t}$), we omit these here for brevity. Together, these constraints suffice to model the energy condition, useful in several verification settings. 

Many other extensions—-such as multi-dimensional energy bounds and other quantitative payoff constraints—-can be modelled similarly using additional constraints or objective functions~\cite{bozzelly:2020,Fijalkow:2014,Mogavero:2015,Weinert:2016}. Further work is needed to precisely define each requirement and establish the appropriate constraints.

\section{Conclusions}
We have introduced a novel constraint-based approach for parity games which does not require integer-based progress measures. The experiments show that our method significantly outperforms existing constraint-based approaches reducing runtime significantly, and improving on memory consumption by over 95\% on large instances, achieving much better results in performance (time and space) compared to previous work, and is competitive against non-CP-based solvers. 

Our key innovation, replacing a SAT encoding with a global constraint, not only improves performance but also provides a flexible framework that can be easily extended to accommodate additional constraints, and therefore more complex requirements.  In this paper, we have shown that these improvements can enable practical verification of large-scale systems while maintaining the modularity and expressiveness of CP-based methods using a modern technology. 

Our work goes beyond parity games by easily enabling new solutions to synthesis and providing the flexibility to adapt our core to solve multiplayer games, including more general games (e.g.,  \wregulargames \cite{Chatterjee:2012}). Our results demonstrate that constraint-based approaches with carefully designed global constraints offer a promising direction for practical formal verification tools that balance expressiveness and computational efficiency.
