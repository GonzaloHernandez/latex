\begin{abstract}
Solving parity games is a cornerstone problem in formal verification and synthesis, with significant applications across industrial-scale system design. We present \nocq, an efficient constraint-based toolchain designed to solve parity games under quantitative conditions. By leveraging the inherent duality of zero-sum game players, \nocq implements several constraint programming (CP) techniques that exploit search space symmetries. Our experimental evaluation demonstrates that \nocq performs competitively against state-of-the-art specialized solvers, often matching or exceeding their performance, while maintaining the modularity and flexibility of a CP-based framework. This flexibility allows users to easily incorporate additional constraints to search for preferred solutions, such as average quantitative performance requirements. We describe the tool's architecture, provide a comprehensive performance analysis, and discuss its extensibility. \nocq is open-source and available at: [anonymous]
% \url{https://github.com/gonzalohernandez/nocq}.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Parity games are two-player zero-sum infinite-duration games played on labelled directed graphs. Solving them is fundamental to automated formal verification, specifically model checking and synthesis. While the problem lies in $\mathsf{NP} \cap \mathsf{co\textit{-}NP}$ , a polynomial-time solution remains elusive despite decades of research. Existing algorithms often encounter exponential runtime limitations in practice. One promising approach involves reducing parity games to SAT instances , which ensures that a winning strategy exists for Player Even if and only if the instance is satisfiable.  Building on the CP-based techniques introduced in~\cite{Hernandez:2026:noc} , we present \nocq: a platform that outperforms previous CP approaches by several orders of magnitude and competes effectively with state-of-the-art solvers~\cite{vanDijk:2018,Benerecetti:2025,Aggarwal:2023}. \nocq extends these algorithms to support quantitative restrictions, such as energy~\cite{ChatterjeeD12} and mean-payoff~\cite{ChatterjeeHS17} constraints, within a unified SAT/CP-based framework. This tool paper describes the architecture, implementation, and usage of \nocq, and provides experimental results demonstrating its efficiency in solving games with these additional quantitative requirements. 

\section{Preliminaries and Overview}

This section presents the graph games that \nocq solves, and some translations into Boolean Satisfiability (SAT) and Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT).

%\subsection{Arenas, Strategies, and Plays}
\subsubsection{Arenas, Strategies, and Plays.}

A game arena is a tuple $\mathcal{A} = (V_{\subeven}, V_{\subodd}, E, \Omega, Q)$, where $V = V_{\subeven} \cup V_{\subodd}$ is a finite set of vertices partitioned between Player \even ($0$) and Player \odd ($1$), $E \subseteq V \times V$ is a set of directed edges where every vertex has at least one successor, $\Omega: V \to \{0, \dots, d\}$ is a priority mapping, and $Q: E \to \mathbb{Z}$ is a weight function.
%
A \textit{positional strategy} for player $i \in \{\even, \odd\}$ is a function $f_{i} : V_{i} \to V$ such that $(v, f_i(v)) \in E$. A pair of strategies $(f_{\subeven}, f_{\subodd})$ uniquely determines an infinite path $\pi = v_0 v_1 \dots$, called a \textit{play}. We denote by $\text{Inf}(\pi)$ the set of priorities $\{\Omega(v) \mid v \text{ occurs infinitely often in } \pi\}$.

\subsubsection{Winning Conditions.}
A play $\pi$ is evaluated against three different criteria:
%
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Parity ($\omega$):} Satisfied if $\max(\text{Inf}(\pi))$ is even.
    \item \textbf{Energy ($\eta$):} For $E_{init} \in \mathbb{N}$, satisfied if $\forall n \ge 0: E_{init} + \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} Q(v_i, v_{i+1}) \ge 0$.
    \item \textbf{Mean-Payoff ($\mu$):} Satisfied if $\liminf_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} Q(v_i, v_{i+1}) \ge 0$.
\end{enumerate}

We define Player \even's winning goal as the combined condition formed by the conjunction of these requirements:
$\gamma_{\subeven} = \omega \wedge \eta \wedge \mu$. As these are zero-sum games, Player \odd wins if the play satisfies $\gamma_{\subodd} = \neg \gamma_{\subeven}$.
%
Given an initial vertex $v_0$, a strategy is said to be winning from $v_0$ for Player~$\even$ (Player~$\odd$) if, starting at $v_0$, it only generates winning plays for Player~$\even$ (Player~$\odd$), for all strategies of the other player. It is known that for every vertex~$v\in V$ in these games, one of the two players always has a winning strategy from that vertex~$v$. Solving a zero-sum game is deciding, for every~$v\in V$, which player has a winning strategy from that vertex.


\subsubsection{Solving Quantitative Parity Games.}
We solve quantitative parity games by encoding them into a Constraint Satisfaction Problem (CSP) with a specialized propagator~\cite{Hernandez:2026:noc}, as follows: 

\begin{definition}\label{def:csp}
    The CSP for an arena $\mathcal{A}$
    %$G=(V,E, \Omega, Q)$ 
    starting at $v_0$ from the perspective of player $\player$ is the triple $\mathcal{P} = (\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{D}, \mathcal{C})$, where $\mathcal{X} = \{ \mathcal{V}_v \}_{v \in V} \cup \{ \mathcal{E}_e \}_{e \in E}$ are Boolean variables $\mathcal{D}$, and the constraints $\mathcal{C}$ are defined in the following way:
    \begin{itemize}
        % [leftmargin=1.5cm, labelwidth=1.2cm, align=right, itemsep=0.2ex, parsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
        [leftmargin=3.3cm, labelwidth=2.8cm, align=right, itemsep=0.2ex, parsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]

        \item [$\mathcal{C}_{v_0}$ :] $\mathcal{V}_{v_0} = \top$

        \item [$\mathcal{C}_{\player}$ :] $\bigwedge_{v \in V_{\player}} (\mathcal{V}_v \rightarrow (\sum_{w \in vE} \mathcal{E}_{(v,w)}= 1))$

        \item [$\mathcal{C}_{\opponent}$ :] $\bigwedge_{v \in V_{\opponent}} (\mathcal{V}_v \rightarrow \bigwedge_{w \in vE} \mathcal{E}_{(v,w)})$

        \item [$\mathcal{C}_{\!E}$ :] $\bigwedge_{w \in V, w \neq v_0} ((\bigvee_{v \in Ew} \mathcal{E}_{(v,w)}) \rightarrow \mathcal{V}_w)$

        \item [$\mathcal{C}_{NOC}(f)$ :] $\bigwedge_{v_0, \dots, v_k} \left( \left(\bigwedge_{i=0}^{k-1} \mathcal{E}_{(v_i,v_{i+1})}\right) \to \bigwedge_{j=0}^{k-1} ((v_j = v_k) \to f(v_j, \dots, v_k)) \right)$

    \end{itemize}
    %
    \begin{itemize}
        [leftmargin=3.3cm, labelwidth=2.8cm, align=right, itemsep=0.5ex, parsep=0pt, topsep=1pt]
        \item [$f_\omega(v_j, \dots, v_k) =$] $\max\{\Omega(v_i) \mid i\in j\dots k\} \bmod 2 \ne \opponent$

        \item [$f_\eta(v_j, \dots, v_k) =$] $\sum_{i=j}^{k-1} Q(v_i, v_{i+1}) \ge 0$

        \item [$f_\mu(v_j, \dots, v_k) =$] $\frac{1}{k-j} \sum_{i=j}^{k-1} Q(v_i, v_{i+1}) \ge \nu$
    \end{itemize}
\end{definition}

$\mathcal{C}_{v_0}, \mathcal{C}_{\player}, \mathcal{C}_{\opponent}$, and $\mathcal{C}_{\!E}$ enforce connectivity --ensuring valid moves for Player \even and \odd, respectively--, where $\player$ select exactly one successor and $\opponent$ should select all of them.  

Moreover, $\mathcal{C}_{\!N\!O\!C}$ ensures that every reachable cycle is winning for $\player$ by satisfying the given combination of parity ($\omega$), energy ($\eta$), and mean-payoff ($\mu$) requirements. Lastly, in~$f_\mu$, parameter~$\nu \in \mathbb{Z}$ is a given performance threshold used to evaluate the mean-payoff condition. This generalized construction allows \nocq to search from the perspective of either player by swapping $\player$ and $\opponent$. Satisfiability under $\player$ proves a winning strategy for $\player$, while unsatisfiability (if the solver explores the full space) proves the win for $\opponent$.

\subsubsection{A No-Opponent-Cycle filter.} \label{sec:nocfilter}
We have developed a complete propagator based on Depth-first Search (DFS) to explore the arena. The resulting DFS tree captures all possible paths from $v_0$, and can be used to detect all existing cycles. We define the propagator in the context of a \textit{Lazy Clause Generation} solver~\cite{chuffed:2018}, to take advantage of the strong performance of this type of solver, in particular for problems that contain a very large number of Boolean clauses. Algorithm~\ref{alg:noc-filter} presents this version of the propagator, which checks for cycles and reports failure if a cycle 
%with highest priority in favour of 
satisfying the winning condition for 
$\opponent$ is detected.

\begin{algorithm}[htb]
\small
\caption{\textbf{\textit{NOCFilter}}}
\textbf{Input}: $path_V,\ path_E,\ v,\ e,\ \mathcal{E},\ \textit{defined}$ \\
\textbf{Output}: $\top$ is valid; $\bot$ otherwise
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \IF{$v \in path_V$} \label{alg:noc-filter:pathcheck}
        \STATE $i_v \gets \textit{index}(v, path_V)$
        \IF{$\neg \gamma_{\player}$} \label{alg:noc-filter:condition}
        % \IF{$\neg \gamma_{\even} \lor \neg \gamma_{\odd}$}
        % \IF{($\player = \even \land \neg(\omega \land \eta \land \mu)) \lor (\player = \odd \land (\omega \land \eta \land \mu)$)}
        % \IF{$\neg satisfyConditions(i_v, path_V, path_E)$} \label{alg:noc-eager:checkopponent}
            \STATE $\textit{reason} \gets \textit{clausify}(\neg path_E[i_v:])$ \label{alg:noc-eager:reason}
            \STATE \textbf{if }not ($\mathcal{E}[e] \gets (\bot, \textit{reason}))$ \textbf{then return $\bot$} \label{alg:noc-eager:ruleoutedge}
        \ENDIF
    \ELSIF{$\textit{defined}$} \label{alg:noc-eager:defcheck}
        \FOR{$e' \in \textit{edges}(v) \textbf{ where } \mathcal{E}[e'] \ne \bot$} \label{alg:no-opponent-cycle-f1:edgecheck}
            \STATE $p_V \gets path_V \cup \{v\}$
            \STATE $p_E \gets path_E \cup \{e'\}$
            \STATE $w \gets \textit{target}(e')$
            \STATE $\textit{def} \gets \mathcal{E}[e'] = \top$
            \STATE \textit{NOCEager}(
                $p_V$,
                $p_E$,
                $w$,
                $e'$,
                $\mathcal{E}$,
                $\textit{def}$\ )
        \ENDFOR
    \ENDIF
    \RETURN $\top$
\end{algorithmic}
\label{alg:noc-filter}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:noc-filter} implements the \noc propagator using a recursive DFS traversal. It maintains the current exploration state via $path_V$ and $path_E$. Parameter $\mathcal{E}$ tracks the Boolean decision variables for arena edges. The core logic occurs when a cycle is detected (Line~\ref{alg:noc-filter:pathcheck}). The propagator evaluates $\gamma_{\player}$---the specific combination of parity ($\omega$), energy ($\eta$), and mean-payoff ($\mu$) objectives in the game. 
%
If the cycle violates the winning criteria for $\player$, the propagator rules out the edge that closes the cycle (Line~\ref{alg:noc-eager:ruleoutedge}), explained by a \textit{conflict clause} generated in Line~\ref{alg:noc-eager:reason} consisting of the negation of the edges currently in $path_E$. This clause effectively {\em learns} the failing partial assignment, allowing the underlying SAT/CP solver to perform non-chronological backtracking whenever this assignment is part of a failure and prune all other search branches that can lead to the same non-winning cycle.

\section{Tool Description}

In this section, we describe the implementation of \nocq, a C++ framework developed to model and solve parity games with quantitative conditions. Unlike \emph{Oink}~\cite{vanDijk:2018}, which focuses on explicit-state systems and symbolic algorithms, \nocq is designed to leverage Constraint Programming (CP) and SAT technologies. The source code, implementation details, usage instructions, and a number of examples are available at [anonymous]
% \url{https://github.com/gonzalohernandez/nocq}.

\subsection*{Core Architecture and Modules}

\nocq is organised into four independent modules: \emph{Game Manager}, \emph{CPSModels}, \emph{SATEncoding}, and \emph{Utils}. This modular design ensures flexibility, allowing the tool to be used either as a standalone CP/SAT solver or as a reusable library.


\subsubsection*{Game Manager.}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:nocq-1}, the \texttt{Game} class serves as the core data structure of \nocq. It captures essential game attributes, including vertex ownership (\texttt{owners}), priorities (\texttt{priors}), and edge weights (\texttt{weights}). In addition, it maintains the arena topology through \texttt{sources} and \texttt{targets} arrays for all edges.

\paragraph{Input and Procedural Generation.}
The framework supports standard formats used to represent games, such as GM~\cite{Parys:2019}, PG~\cite{vanDijk:2018}, and DZN~\cite{Minizinc:2020}. We also define the GMW (\texttt{.gmw}) format, an extension of GM specifically designed to store edge weights.
%
Moreover, 
%\paragraph{Procedural Generation.}
following Parys~\cite{Parys:2019}, \texttt{Game} supports the procedural generation of \emph{Random}, \emph{Jurdziński}, and \emph{MLadder} games for benchmarking purposes.

Users can interact with the Game Manager either to load existing benchmarks or to generate new game instances. The first command below loads a parity game and assigns random edge weights in the range $[-20,20]$; by default, the solver maximizes for the parity reward. The second line generates a Jurdziński game with 15{,}000 levels and 50 blocks, set to minimize the reward:

\begin{lstlisting}
$ ./nocq --gm /pgsolver/randomgame-20000-10-1-20-0.gm --weights -20 20
$ ./nocq --jurd 15000 50 --weights -50 30 --min
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection*{Constraint-Based Solving (CPSModels).}

Figure~\ref{fig:nocq-2} illustrates how \nocq implements the CSP model in Definition~\ref{def:csp} and integrates the \noc propagators described in Section~\ref{sec:nocfilter}. A key design principle of \nocq is model independence: the underlying model can be interfaced with different constraint programming (CP) engines.
%
Once a game is loaded or created, an instance of \texttt{NOCModel} is instantiated and used to solve the problem. The \texttt{NOCModel} searches for solutions by enforcing all constraints, including $C_{\text{NOC}}$ from Definition~\ref{def:csp}. This constraint is implemented through \texttt{NOCPropagator}, which uses CP techniques to evaluate winning conditions over all relevant cycles in the input game.

In the current version, three different winning conditions are implemented. The system is extensible to incorporate new winning conditions by extending the \texttt{WinningCondition} class with additional objectives. The framework has been validated using two state-of-the-art solvers:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chuffed}~\cite{chuffed:2018}, a lazy clause generation solver.
    \item \textbf{Gecode}~\cite{Gecode:2019}, a comprehensive constraint-based toolkit.
\end{itemize}

Once a game has been loaded or created, one can invoke the constraint-based solver from the command line. Several parameters are available to tailor the solving process. The following command solves a parity+energy game (threshold=20) from a given initial vertex (18), and reports the execution times:

\begin{lstlisting}
$ ./nocq --gm /games/rand_50_20_1_5_-20_30.gmw --noc --init 18
    --parity --energy 20 --print-times
\end{lstlisting}

    % --weights -50 50 --parity --mean-payoff 20 --print-times

    The architecture of \nocq is very modular, allowing new CP solvers to be integrated into the whole toolchain using the model developed in~\cite{Hernandez:2026:noc}.

\subsubsection*{SAT Encoding.}

\nocq also provides a dedicated SAT encoding pipeline, enabling parity games to be dispatched to a number of high-performance SAT solvers, including \emph{CaDiCaL}~\cite{Biere:Kissat:2024}, \emph{Kissat}~\cite{Biere:Kissat:2024}, and \emph{MiniSat}~\cite{Een:MiniSat:2003}. Figure~\ref{fig:nocq-3} illustrates the \texttt{SATEncoder} class, which implements the encoding techniques proposed by Heljanko {\em et al}~\cite{Heljanko:2012}.

The \texttt{SATEncoder} maintains references to the \texttt{Game} instance and maps graph components to integer representations of vertices ($V$) and edges ($E$). The module produces standard CNF output in DIMACS format, ensuring compatibility with any compliant SAT solver. We note that the SAT reduction proposed in~\cite{Heljanko:2012} is restricted to parity objectives/games only, and does not support quantitative winning conditions such as energy or mean-payoff, as we do within \nocq.

The following example encodes the \texttt{towersofhanoi-5} game from the pgsolver collection~\cite{Friedmann:2017}, starting from vertex~15, into CNF format. The encoding targets reward minimization, as described in~\cite{Heljanko:2012}. The resulting output, a DIMACS file, can then be passed onto any SAT solver, such as CaDiCaL:

\begin{lstlisting}
$ ./nocq --gm /pgsolver/towersofhanoi-5.gm --sat-encoding tower5.cnf --init 15
$ ./cadical tower5.cnf
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection*{Auxiliary Algorithms (Utils).}

Beyond CP and SAT technologies, \nocq includes procedures for evaluation and preprocessing, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:nocq-4}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Zielonka}~\cite{Aggarwal:2023}: parity games algorithm with improved attractor computation.
    \item \textbf{TarjanSCC}~\cite{Tarjan:1972}: efficient SCC decomposition of game arenas.
\end{itemize}

The command below generates a random game (20 vertices, 10 priorities, degree 1–5), solves it using the Zielonka Recursive Algorithm (ZRA), and exports the arena to \texttt{rand.gm}, subsequently performing SCC decomposition:

\begin{lstlisting}
$ ./nocq --rand 20 10 1 5 --zra --print-solution --export-gm rand.gm
$ ./nocq --gm rand.gm --scc
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent \nocq users can view the full list of supported parameters using \texttt{./nocq -\-help}. A more in-depth explanation of specific configurations and advanced usage is available as part of the documentation in the Git repository of \nocq. 

\subsubsection{Exploiting Problem Duality through Parallel Solving.}

The performance of game solvers is strongly influenced by the topological and quantitative structure of the arena. A key observation is the \emph{asymmetry in computational cost}: game instances that are difficult to solve from the perspective of player~$\player$ are often easier (in practical terms) from the perspective of $\opponent$, and vice versa.
%
To exploit this duality, \nocq executes the CSP concurrently from both perspectives in separate threads. The computation terminates as soon as one thread identifies a winning strategy or proves its absence. This parallel approach mitigates worst-case behavior by leveraging the more computationally favorable viewpoint, which depends on the particular game that is given to \nocq as input.

\section{Experiments and Evaluation}\label{sec:experiments}

In this section, we evaluate \nocq across two dimensions: qualitative parity games and quantitative multi-objective verification. All experiments were conducted on an Intel Xeon 8260 CPU with 24 cores (non-hyperthreaded) and 268.55 GB of RAM running Linux.


\subsection{Parity conditions}

First, we assess its performance on games featuring parity conditions only, comparing \nocq against state-of-the-art algorithms using standard benchmarks.

For the SAT backends, we employ several high-performance solvers: \textbf{CaDiCaL} (v2.1.3) \cite{Biere:Kissat:2024}, \textbf{MiniSat} (v2.2.0) \cite{Een:MiniSat:2003}, and \textbf{Kissat} (v4.0.2) \cite{Biere:Kissat:2024}. To establish a robust baseline, we compare our results with \textbf{Oink} \cite{vanDijk:2018}, which implements state-of-the-art algorithms such as Priority Promotion ($\text{PP}$ and $\text{PP+}$) and Parys' improvements ($\text{PAR}$). Additionally, we include Zielonka's Recursive Algorithm ($\text{ZRA}$) featuring the optimized attractor strategy presented in \cite{Aggarwal:2023}, which is not yet integrated into Oink.

The benchmarks used were taken from the PGSolver Library \cite{Friedmann:2017} and Keiren's benchmarks for parity games \cite{Keiren:2015}. The evaluation utilizes 469 benchmark instances categorized into four groups: \textbf{Equivalence Checking (EqC)}, containing 77 instances from formal verification tasks with complex structural connectivity; \textbf{Model Checking (MoC)}, consisting of 106 organic benchmarks derived from $\mu$-calculus problems ; the \textbf{PGSolver Collection (PGS)}, with 161 games from difficult families designed to test theoretical worst-case bounds ; and \textbf{Random Games (RaG)}, comprising 125 instances with varied priority distributions used to assess average-case efficiency and general robustness.

We focus our evaluation on large-scale benchmarks, which provide a more rigorous test for the scalability of \nocq against dedicated solvers. This category includes arenas with up to 35 million vertices , representing real-world verification challenges where traditional SAT-based methods often fail to scale. By prioritizing these high-complexity instances, we demonstrate \nocq's robustness in industrial-scale formal verification tasks.

\subsection{Quantitative Goals and Verification}

One of \nocq's main features is the ability to handle quantitative goals within the same CP-based framework. 
%
\nocq implements a core algorithm designed to compute positional strategies, which are optimal in case only qualitative (parity) or only quantitative goals are given. However, when combined, positional strategies may no longer be optimal~\cite{ChatterjeeHJ05,Chatterjee:2010}. As such, the solutions that \nocq computes are for games where only positional strategies are allowed. 
%
In this context, the next set of benchmarks demonstrates \nocq's performance when solving such kinds of games when enriched with additional quantitative (energy and/or mean-payoff) requirements.  
%
To this end, we solve a selection of random benchmarks as proposed in \cite{Chaloupka:2011}, following the design of the \texttt{SPRAND} generator \cite{CherkasskyBoris:1996} for energy and mean-payoff games. These benchmarks utilize random positional vertex assignments and randomly generated thresholds. We assess the tool's efficiency across various multi-objective configurations defined by the winning condition $\gamma_{p} = \omega \otimes \eta \otimes \mu$, where $\otimes$ denotes the product composition of parity ($\omega$), energy ($\eta$), and mean-payoff ($\mu$) objectives.

The benchmarks are categorized by their structural properties using the naming convention \texttt{R\_<d><s\_key>}, where \texttt{<d>} represents the out-degree (density) and \texttt{<s\_key>} denotes the state-space size. We consider two density levels: low density (5) and high density (10). The vertex counts are scaled as follows: $m = 2^{16}$, $b = 2^{17}$, $h = 2^{18}$, $xb = 2^{19}$, and $xh = 2^{20}$.

For single-objective instances, the parity condition ($\omega$) remains consistently the easiest to solve, maintaining a 100\% success rate regardless of graph size or density. In contrast, energy ($\eta$) and mean-payoff ($\mu$) objectives exhibit greater sensitivity to state-space scaling; as the vertex count increases, there is a noticeable decline in the number of instances solved within the time limit.

A key strength of the \nocq toolchain is highlighted in the multi-objective configurations. When combining parity with an energy objective (e.g., $\omega \otimes \eta$), the solver frequently demonstrates a pruning effect, where the parity condition helps narrow the search space, leading to more robust solving times compared to other combinations. However, for the most complex triple-objective configurations ($\omega \otimes \eta \otimes \mu$), the performance tends to be bottlenecked by the mean-payoff component.

Nevertheless, our results for energy games show a reasonable solving time compared with their reported results.  Furthermore, obtaining standardized benchmarks for this class of games remains a challenge, as few publicly available datasets or implementations exist.

\section{Conclusions and Related Work} 

As demonstrated by our experimental evaluation, \nocq is an efficient toolchain that leverages a constraint-based approach to solve games on graphs with performance competitive with state-of-the-art algorithms used in formal verification to solve parity games. A key advantage of \nocq lies in its ability to solve parity games combined with energy and mean-payoff conditions simultaneously, maintaining high performance without increasing implementation complexity.
%
Because \nocq is built upon CP, the model is inherently extensible; new constraints can be added seamlessly without modifying the underlying solver architecture. 

Finally, several tools have been developed for quantitative games only -- {\em e.g.}, see~\cite{BenerecettiDM24} and references therein. But, much less work has been done regarding practical tools for parity games (or $\omega$-regular games more generally) with additional quantitative requirements. 
%
In~\cite{ChatterjeeHJS11,DziadekFS25} two attempts were made, but in such cases full parity games with quantitative objectives were not evaluated in practice. Our results provide a step forward where the case for positional strategies is accounted for in complex parity games with additional quantitative requirements.
